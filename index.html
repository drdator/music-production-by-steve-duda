Music Production by Steve Duda

An editorial guide synthesized from Steve Duda's three-part Pyramind session. This is not a verbatim transcript or a text written by Duda. It organizes his demonstrations, opinions, and working methods into a practical production workflow.

Who is Steve Duda?

Steve Duda is a producer, audio engineer, sound designer, and self-taught software developer whose career sits at the intersection of music and technology. His work spans studio projects with artists including Nine Inch Nails and A Perfect Circle, electronic releases with deadmau5 as BSOD, and the founding of Xfer Records. He is best known for creating Serum and a family of widely used production tools.

Steve Duda's approach to music production is built around a simple idea: a clear result usually comes from deciding what matters and removing what competes with it. Tools are useful, but they are secondary to hierarchy, timing, and intent.

His recurring questions are:

  • What is the listener supposed to notice right now?
  • What can be quieter, shorter, or absent?
  • Which sound owns the low end at this moment?
  • Does this part help the song, or merely occupy space?
  • Can the problem be solved with level before reaching for a plugin?

The rest of this guide develops those questions into a repeatable method.

1. Begin with intent

Before changing a mix, listen to the whole song and then stop it. In the silence, imagine the best version of what you just heard. Do not begin by inventorying defects. Ask how the song could feel exciting, clear, and inevitable when it comes out of the speakers.

This creates a target. Without one, production becomes a sequence of disconnected reactions: browse presets, add processing, fix the processing, and keep moving without knowing what the result should be.

You do not need to preserve every choice equally. Preserve the musical identity, then place it in the best possible light.

A useful first pass

  1. Listen without touching anything.
  2. Stop playback and imagine the ideal impact, balance, and low-end movement.
  3. Name the focal element in each section.
  4. Identify the elements that support it.
  5. Decide what can be reduced rather than enhanced.

2. Build the hierarchy with levels

Duda describes mixing as overwhelmingly a matter of level balance. A strong mix can be built with faders alone because level establishes foreground, middle ground, and background.

Start with no corrective processing if possible. Balance the song so the listener never has to decode twelve equally important parts. The mix should guide attention.

At any moment, decide which element is:

  • Foreground: the principal information—the vocal, hook, lead, or important rhythmic event.
  • Middle ground: the musical support that gives the foreground context.
  • Background: texture, atmosphere, and secondary motion.

The background is allowed to sound incomplete in solo. It only needs to perform its job in the mix.

Introduce, then settle

When a new lead or featured sound enters, briefly place it a little louder than its eventual level. Once the ear has identified it, bring it back into the mix. The listener tends to follow it downward while continuing to perceive it as important.

This avoids the common escalation in which the lead is raised, then the drums are raised to compete, then everything hits the master harder. Use automation to direct attention instead of making the entire arrangement louder.

3. Create space by subtraction

Clarity is largely the absence of unwanted material. Rather than asking what to add to a sound, ask what you can afford to remove.

Three forms of subtraction solve different problems:

  1. Lower the level. This should usually be the first move.
  2. Shorten the sound. Remove energy after the sound has delivered its information.
  3. Remove unnecessary frequencies. Use EQ after level and duration are working.

This order matters. Frequency conflicts are often level conflicts in disguise, and crowded arrangements are often duration problems rather than EQ problems.

The importance of time

Time is one of the main dimensions of a mix. A sound occupies space for as long as it continues. Once a percussive event has defined the beat, its sustain may add little beyond clutter.

For drums and other transient material:

  • Preserve or emphasize the initial hit.
  • Reduce the sustain if it masks the next event.
  • Use the source envelope when producing the part.
  • When working from stems, use volume shaping or expansion to reconstruct the desired envelope.

This can make percussion feel punchier even though the sound has less total energy. Compression is not automatically the route to weight; an over-compressed sample may need more contrast between transient and body, not less.

4. Use EQ according to a sound's role

The more prominent and naturally good a sound is, the less aggressively Duda tends to EQ it. A well-recorded foreground element can often be left broad and intact. A quiet background element can be filtered much more drastically because only one part of its character may matter.

For example, if a background percussion sound contributes only useful midrange texture, its lows and highs do not need to survive merely because they existed in the source.

Midrange congestion

The midrange carries identity, melody, words, and much of the information needed to distinguish instruments. When it feels crowded:

  1. Revisit levels.
  2. Revisit the timing and sustain of competing parts.
  3. Decide which element should win.
  4. Remove frequencies from the supporting element rather than boosting the focal element by default.

If a vocal lacks intelligibility, boosting its mids may make it harsh or nasal. Lowering or scooping the competing synth can expose the vocal without changing the vocal's character.

Surgical EQ

Use narrow notches for a specific audible defect, such as an isolated ring in a kick—not as a routine way to sculpt every source. Ears, rooms, and speakers all have irregular responses, so extreme surgery can accidentally compensate for the monitoring system rather than the music.

A practical way to locate an offending resonance is to sweep a narrow bell boost until the unwanted tone becomes unmistakable, then cut it. The decision should begin with hearing the problem, not with seeing it on an analyzer.

Read analyzers carefully

A spectrum analyzer trades time resolution against frequency resolution. A larger analysis window shows more frequency detail but describes a longer slice of time. A smaller window updates faster but resolves frequency less precisely.

This matters for transient sounds. A kick can display broad high-frequency energy that exists only for an instant at its attack. A static spectrum does not explain when that energy happens. Use the analyzer to learn what a sound contains, but use your ears to judge its role over time.

5. Give the low end one owner

Duda's rule of thumb is that a track can have a huge kick or a huge bass at the same moment, but usually not both. In the lowest octaves, two fundamentals do not remain neatly separate. Their phase relationship produces reinforcement, cancellation, and inconsistent weight.

The goal is not simply to tune both sounds to the same note. Matching fundamentals can still turn two intended sources into one unstable composite wave.

Choose by genre and intent

  • In house-oriented music, the kick often owns the deepest low end and acts as the physical timekeeper. The bass can be more syncopated and midrange-rich.
  • In bass-oriented music, the bass may own the sub range. The kick can communicate size through punch, upper harmonics, or reverb rather than deep sustained energy.

These are starting points, not laws. The real decision is which source should move the subwoofer at each moment.

Separate kick and bass in time

If both need low-frequency weight, use arrangement, envelope shaping, or sidechain control so their fundamentals do not dominate simultaneously. Ducking the bass when the kick hits is one implementation; shortening either source is another.

To audit the result, low-pass the master around 120 Hz and listen only to the low-frequency motion. Ask:

  • Is the sub rhythm deliberate?
  • Are distinct impacts separated by moments of relief?
  • Does the low end pulse, or has it become a continuous standing tone?
  • Does each new kick feel like a new event?

Design for the room

Low frequencies continue to propagate and reverberate in a club after the waveform in the file has ended. A kick that sounds acceptably long on headphones can become a constant rumble over a large system. Shortening the source leaves room for the venue and lets the next hit regain impact.

Suggest size without constant sub energy

The impression of size can come from more than fundamentals:

  • Reverb can make a kick appear physically large without filling the sub range.
  • Harmonics can make a bass feel aggressive and present even when its deepest frequencies are reduced.
  • Sidechain movement can imply that a source is powerful enough to push the rest of the track away.
  • Contrast makes the arrival of genuine sub energy feel larger.

The listener's brain calibrates to context. Use that calibration instead of asking every source to be objectively enormous.

6. Treat vocals and leads as information

A listener should not have to strain to understand the words or follow the lead. Duda compares an unclear mix to trying to hear a friend in a noisy restaurant: the brain can perform the separation, but the experience is tiring.

To improve intelligibility:

  • Lower the competing part.
  • Remove competing midrange from supporting elements.
  • Automate other parts down during the vocal.
  • Sidechain a competing instrument from the vocal so it recedes during phrases and returns between them.
  • Let the lead enter prominently, then settle once identified.

The goal is high contrast. Let the most important part win decisively.

7. Organize the drums around their function

Duda often keeps the kick outside the main drum bus. The kick can then remain a stable, independent anchor while the rest of the kit is contained together.

A useful layout is:

  • Kick: independent, especially when it owns the low end.
  • Drum bus: snare, claps, hats, percussion, and other kit elements as appropriate.
  • Optional percussion bus: useful when the kick, snare, and claps need more independence.

The bus is not necessarily there to make everything “fat.” It can simply establish a dynamic boundary and make the parts feel as if they inhabit the same place. Bus processing should follow an intention: containment, cohesion, color, or controlled peaks.

8. Use a simple mix architecture

One of Duda's working layouts reduces a complex mix to three principal controls:

  1. Kick
  2. Bass
  3. Everything else

The third path is a submaster containing the rest of the music. This makes the central balance easy to inspect and allows processing of the broader mix without forcing the low-end anchors through every stage.

There is no universal routing scheme. Buses are useful when they create a meaningful control point, not because every mix must follow a template.

Gain staging

Duda's session reflects an in-the-box workflow with floating-point headroom, but he warns against assuming every plugin behaves safely above 0 dBFS. Some processors—especially nonlinear or analog-modeled ones—change behavior according to input level.

The durable principle is simple: leave intentional margin before plugins and control the level entering the master chain. Do not rely on an arbitrary kick setting as a substitute for checking the actual signal path.

9. Mix into the intended final behavior

Duda commonly mixes through a master chain rather than waiting until the end to discover how mastering changes the balance. His exact chain varies, but the concepts include:

  • A small amount of stereo widening early enough that the mix is balanced into it.
  • One or more compressors with distinct purposes.
  • Parallel compression to bring up quieter detail rather than only pushing peaks down.
  • A final limiter that may catch roughly 1–2 dB on the kick while leaving most other events alone.

When the kick triggers light limiting, the mix gains a subtle pumping effect: the kick appears dominant while the rest of the song momentarily gives way. This is different from obvious sidechain pumping and should remain deliberate and restrained.

Do not copy a chain blindly. Save successful chains from previous projects and use them as starting points. Different songs may respond dramatically differently to the same processing, so audition several relevant starting points and choose by result.

Width belongs in the mix decision

Master-bus widening changes the balance between mid and side information. If added only after the mix is finished, a wide pad or ambience may become louder relative to centered material. Establish modest width before final level decisions so the balance accounts for it.

10. Compare fairly and often

Keep the rough mix or reference mix available on a dedicated track. Route it so it bypasses your master processing, and assign a single control to switch between it and your work.

Match playback level closely enough that louder does not automatically win. The successful mix should feel clearer, punchier, and more open while preserving the song's intent.

The ideal achievement is not for the listener to admire the mix. It is for the listener to notice the tune without being distracted by the mix.

11. Make music people want to turn up

Duda's loudness principle is to make a song that people want to hear loud, not a file that is intrinsically loud. Excessive limiting can make turning up the volume unpleasant because the track reaches the threshold of pain before it delivers physical contrast.

If a DJ set requires an aggressively limited version to compete with neighboring tracks, create a separate performance master. Do not automatically impose that compromise on the release version. The listener already has a volume control; dynamics can make using it rewarding.

12. Sound design: clarity begins at the source

Duda connects mix clarity to oscillator and playback quality. Unwanted aliasing creates frequencies that were not part of the intended sound. Distortion and multiband compression can raise those artifacts until they become audible clutter.

The broader production lesson is not that every synth must be pristine. It is that source quality determines what later processing exaggerates. Know whether grit is intentional.

Design visually, decide aurally

Serum's visual modulation and wavetable feedback grew from Duda's desire to understand what a control is doing. A visual interface can make modulation easier to reason about, but the display serves the ear rather than replacing it.

When designing a sound:

  1. Begin with the sonic role, not a maximal patch.
  2. Choose a source with the right harmonic material.
  3. Shape its duration and movement.
  4. Add only the modulation needed to express the idea.
  5. Check the result in context.

Wavetable synthesis is valuable because a sample can be divided into individual cycles, frozen, scanned in either direction, looped, or interpolated into frames that did not exist in the source. This turns recorded material into a flexible oscillator rather than a fixed playback event.

Also respect the instrument's limits. A wavetable synth is not automatically the right tool for every sound; a convincing acoustic piano, for example, may call for multisampling or physical modeling.

13. Work fast enough to preserve the idea

From working with deadmau5, Duda observed a decisive sample-selection process: know roughly what you want, move quickly through options, and stop when one is close enough. Do not spend an hour turning the first kick into the greatest isolated kick ever.

If each part reaches roughly 80 percent of the original vision, the combination can reveal the full intent through interaction. Obsessing over one element too early risks losing the whole track.

This suggests a two-speed workflow:

Composition speed

  • Hear the target internally.
  • Choose sounds quickly.
  • Build all essential parts.
  • Keep moving while the musical idea is alive.

Refinement speed

  • Reassess hierarchy.
  • Shorten and subtract.
  • Correct genuine defects.
  • Automate attention through time.
  • Finish loose ends against a clear checklist.

14. Build your own solutions

Duda's career repeatedly follows the same pattern: encounter a practical limitation, understand it well enough to describe the desired behavior, then build a tool or workflow that removes the friction.

This does not require mastering a field before starting. His learning method was example-driven:

  1. Find a working example whose result you understand.
  2. Examine the parts that produce it.
  3. Change one thing.
  4. Compile, listen, and observe.
  5. Repeat in small steps.

Large projects become manageable in the same way. Work on one local problem at a time. Eventually, zoom out, collect the unfinished edges into a concrete list, and impose a shipping boundary. A creative tool can remain updateable without remaining unreleased forever.

15. Develop a voice, not an imitation

Duda's closing advice extends beyond engineering. Your background and experience are usable material, not disadvantages to erase. An idol is more likely to be interested in a distinct voice than in a faithful copy of work that already exists.

Success should also be defined broadly enough to survive contact with reality. A dream job can become repetitive once it is ordinary. Aim for a day-to-day life that keeps you learning, meeting people, and working near the intersection that genuinely interests you.

Multiple skills create more routes into that life: production, mixing, sound design, software, teaching, engineering, or combinations no established job title captures.

A compact Duda-style production checklist

Before processing

  • What should the song feel like at its best?
  • What is the focal element in this section?
  • Can I establish the hierarchy with faders alone?

For every crowded sound

  • Can it be quieter?
  • Can it be shorter?
  • Which portion of its spectrum actually matters?
  • Is it foreground, middle ground, or background?

For the low end

  • Does the kick or the bass own the fundamental here?
  • Is the other source making room in time?
  • Does the sub rhythm remain clear below roughly 120 Hz?
  • Will room decay turn this into continuous rumble?

For vocals and leads

  • Is the focal information effortless to understand?
  • Can competing parts move down instead of the lead moving up?
  • Does the entrance attract attention before settling?

For percussion

  • Is the transient clear?
  • Is compression reducing the contrast I need?
  • Does the sustain help, or only occupy time?

Before export

  • Does the master chain receive sensible levels?
  • Is width part of the mix rather than a surprise afterward?
  • Does the limiter control peaks without erasing movement?
  • Does the mix beat the rough at matched loudness?
  • Does the song invite the listener to turn it up?
